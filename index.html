Why Is It So Hard for the U.S. and Iran to Start Peace Talks?

The United States and Iran are both saying they want the war to stop. But they still cannot begin real negotiations. Each side has strong conditions, and both governments do not trust each other.

Original news: CNN (March 25, 2026)

Simple News Story (B1)

The U.S. and Iran are sending messages about the war, but this is not the same as direct peace talks. On March 25, Iranian officials said that Iran rejected a ceasefire idea from the U.S. side. Iran also gave its own demands and said these points must be considered first. Iranian leaders explained that right now there are only messages through other countries, and they do not call this process a true negotiation.

From the U.S. perspective, officials say they still want to reduce fighting and protect their security goals in the region. They also say diplomacy is possible if both sides become more flexible. However, reports suggest that there are serious disagreements about what should happen first. For example, one side may want fighting to stop immediately, while the other side wants political or military promises before accepting a ceasefire.

Another problem is trust. The two governments have had tense relations for many years, and recent events have made this even worse. When trust is low, each message is checked very carefully, and both sides worry that the other side may not keep promises. Because of this, even small progress can take a lot of time.

Experts say that indirect communication can help avoid total breakdown, but it is usually slower than face-to-face talks. If both countries continue to exchange conditions without compromise, a formal peace process may not begin soon. At the same time, analysts note that continued contact is still important. Even limited communication can create a small chance for future negotiations, especially if outside mediators can help both sides find shared interests.

In short, both the U.S. and Iran publicly discuss ending the conflict, but they disagree on the path. Different priorities, hard demands, and deep mistrust are making peace talks difficult. This is why the situation remains uncertain, and why many observers believe diplomacy will require patience and many steps.

Key Points

  • Iran rejected a U.S. ceasefire proposal and presented different conditions.
  • Iran says current communication is only message exchange, not formal negotiation.
  • The U.S. says it still wants to lower violence and keep a diplomatic path open.
  • Major disagreements and low trust are blocking quick progress toward talks.
  • Experts say indirect communication helps, but real agreements may take a long time.

Vocabulary

Ceasefire
An agreement to stop fighting for a period of time.
Demand
Something a person or country says must happen.
Negotiation
Formal talks to reach an agreement.
Guarantee
A clear promise that something will happen.
Mediator
A person or country that helps two sides talk and find agreement.
